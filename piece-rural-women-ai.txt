title: What Will It Take for Rural Women in India to Use AI with Confidence?
date: 2025-12-05
summary: India's efforts to expand AI literacy among rural women must account for a less visible barrier to adoption: the time, confidence and freedom required to learn an unfamiliar technology.
---

For someone accustomed to artificial intelligence, asking a chatbot a question takes seconds. The answer may be useful, inaccurate or utterly absurd. Either way, there is little hesitation in trying again. But consider someone for whom a smartphone is shared household property, mobile data is rationed and an unfamiliar application carries the possibility of an expensive mistake. Experimentation becomes a more complicated decision.

As India brings AI into rural livelihoods programmes, digital inclusion is often measured through connectivity, affordability and technical skills. These matter, but access to a smartphone tells us little about whether someone feels able to use it independently. This question informed our work at the Centre for Social and Behaviour Change (CSBC), Ashoka University, where we designed AI Saheli, an approximately twelve-hour AI-literacy curriculum for 170,000 women leaders from self-help groups (SHGs) in Uttar Pradesh. Our research examined what could stop women from trying AI.

Through the [Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana-National Rural Livelihoods Mission (DAY-NRLM)](https://myscheme.gov.in/schemes/day-nrlm), the government has built a large network of self-help groups to support rural women's livelihoods and economic participation. The Lakhpati Didi initiative extends this ambition, with a target of enabling six crore women to earn an annual household income of at least one lakh rupees by March 2029. For women building and managing small enterprises, AI offers a range of practical applications. A woman running a food-processing business, for instance, could use it to draft a message to a prospective buyer or translate a bank document. Yet, knowing what a tool can do for her business does not necessarily mean feeling confident enough to use it.

## Access Does Not Guarantee Confidence

Our interviews brought up concerns that conventional technology training can easily overlook. Some women had limited control over their smartphones because they shared them with their husbands or sons. Others were hesitant to experiment, worrying that pressing the wrong button might delete photographs, alter settings or compromise a linked bank account. Even spending mobile data on an unfamiliar application could feel like an unnecessary expense.

Privacy and security also emerged as concerns. One Lakhpati Didi, for instance, told us: "I don't know who is actually answering my questions. I worry that if I talk to this AI, it might secretly read my WhatsApp messages or take money from my UPI."

While using smartphones, people learn by selecting options or tapping familiar icons. AI, however, asks them to describe what they want, interpret an unexpected answer and refine the instruction. They must experiment with language and judge a response. And experimentation takes time. [India's Time Use Survey 2024](https://mospi.gov.in/sites/default/files/publication_reports/TUS_Factsheet_25022025.pdf) found that women participating in unpaid domestic services spent an average of 289 minutes daily on these activities, compared with 88 minutes for men. The difference leaves women with fewer opportunities for uninterrupted practice, particularly when they also undertake paid work.

Getting comfortable with an unfamiliar technology takes time. People forget instructions and make mistakes. Yet training sessions alone cannot build familiarity with a tool. Participants need time to use it on their own, return to it when they get stuck and practise without someone guiding every step. Finding that time outside a training session, however, is not easy.

## The Role of Curiosity in Learning

For someone hesitant to use AI, the experience of trying it for the first time can shape whether they return to it. Asking a chatbot to interpret a bank document may feel daunting, but asking it to create an absurd picture is a different proposition. An imperfect image can be laughed at and changed; an inaccurate financial answer is considerably more unsettling.

The curriculum's approach drew on [UTAUT2](https://misq.org/consumer-acceptance-and-use-of-information-technology-extending-the-unified-theory-of-acceptance-and-use-of-technology.html), a framework developed to understand why people adopt and continue using technology. It recognises hedonic motivation, or the enjoyment and pleasure people derive from using a technology. Rather than introducing AI only through its potential economic applications, we wanted participants to first experience it as something they could explore, experiment with and enjoy. We began with playful, low-stakes activities, while gradually building participants' confidence in their ability to use the tools.

We also used the [EAST framework](https://www.bi.team/publications/east-four-simple-ways-to-apply-behavioural-insights/), which encourages behaviours to be made Easy, Attractive, Social and Timely, to shape how these learning experiences were delivered.

## Teach Curiosity Before Utility

AI Saheli comprises four modules: Curiosity, Freedom, Show and Tell, and Safety. Across sixteen hands-on activities, it introduces AI gradually, beginning with experiences in which mistakes carry little consequence.

The first module opens with a blind-sketching exercise in which participants draw their partners without looking at the paper. The inevitably imperfect drawings let people laugh at mistakes before using an unfamiliar application. Being wrong becomes part of the exercise, not evidence of incapability. Participants begin by experimenting in a setting where there is little pressure to get things right. Further, they experiment with image generation, riddles and translation. One activity teaches prompting through Naam, Jagah, Kaam (name, place, action). A participant might ask AI to create an image of a woman eating golgappas on an aeroplane, then add details about her clothes or surroundings and watch the image change. The abstract becomes repeatable and she learns that an AI response need not be final.

Later activities connect AI to participants' ambitions, such as visualising a shop they hope to open, exploring ways to expand an enterprise or understanding an unfamiliar document. Starting with their own goals gives participants control over what they learn.

Drawing on SHG peer-learning structures, participants work together, demonstrate what they have made and share through WhatsApp. Watching another member use a tool can make experimentation feel achievable.

The final module addresses safety. Participants examine suspicious messages, misleading images and deceptive urgency or improbable rewards, then practise verifying information and questioning AI answers. The aim is confidence without blind trust.

## Measure Use (And Not Just Attendance)

If AI literacy expands through rural livelihoods programmes, completion figures will be inadequate. A participant may finish a session and still struggle a month later. AI Saheli includes baseline and endline assessments of smartphone comfort, AI awareness and confidence. Follow-up should examine whether participants continue using the tools after the training, whether they find practical applications for them in their everyday lives and whether they can identify and question unreliable AI-generated information.

Programmes should make room for practice during SHG meetings. Dedicated time, affordable data and peer support reduce reliance on spare time at home. For women who share devices or have limited opportunities to use them independently, the challenge extends beyond learning how to use AI. They need the opportunity to practise and apply what they have learnt outside the training sessions.

SHG leaders and community resource persons could sustain learning beyond initial sessions. AI Saheli's trainer-development model builds on this network through facilitator guidance and peer activities. Scaling will require training quality, especially as participants use AI for consequential decisions.

A curriculum that begins with sketches, riddles and playful images might seem far removed from the larger challenges of digital inclusion. But for women who have had little opportunity to experiment with AI, these activities offer a less intimidating place to start. They can try something, get an unexpected result, laugh about it and try again. Whether this design leads to sustained independent use must be established through implementation and evaluation.

Making AI available to women is only part of the task. They also need the confidence to try it out, the freedom to question what it tells them and a clear sense of where it can be useful. That cannot be achieved through training alone. It also depends on whether women have the time, support and opportunity to keep learning and using these tools in their daily lives.
