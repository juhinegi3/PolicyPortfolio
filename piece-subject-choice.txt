title: Has India's Education Policy Changed Faster Than Its Institutions?
date: 2024-06-24
summary: NEP 2020 promises greater academic flexibility and interdisciplinary learning, but schools, universities and admissions systems have been slower to adapt.
---

For decades, Indian students have been expected to narrow their academic interests at sixteen, choosing between science, commerce and humanities. These choices have often determined which degrees they can pursue, both in India and abroad, long before they have had the opportunity to explore their interests fully.
The National Education Policy (NEP) of 2020 sought to move beyond these divisions by giving students greater freedom to choose subjects across disciplines. Four years later, curriculum reforms are in motion, but their success depends on whether schools can offer these combinations and universities are prepared to recognise them. A student may have greater freedom to choose subjects under the new framework, but the options available to her will still depend on what her school can offer and how universities assess those choices for admission.
In the proposed changes to the school curriculum, the National Curriculum Framework for School Education (2023), envisages moving away from the traditional division of Classes 11 and 12 into science, commerce and humanities, allowing students to combine subjects across disciplines. Changes have also reached the examination hall. For the 2024–25 academic session, the Central Board of Secondary Education increased the proportion of competency-based questions in Classes 11 and 12 from 40 to 50 per cent, placing greater emphasis on applying concepts as opposed to memorising them.
However, allowing students to choose subjects and giving them access to those subjects are two different matters. According to the Ministry of Education's UDISE+ 2021–22 report, only 44.9 per cent of schools had computer facilities, while internet connectivity was available in just 33.9 per cent. These figures might not directly measure the availability of academic subjects, but they reflect the uneven resources with which schools are expected to implement reforms. Offering multidisciplinary combinations requires schools to have qualified teachers, suitable facilities and the capacity to accommodate different subjects within their timetables.
A well-resourced private school in Delhi NCR may be able to offer Physics, Psychology and Economics together, while a government school with limited teaching staff may struggle with the same. The scope for academic exploration thus remains contingent on the resources of individual institutions. Unless these differences are addressed, reforms intended to broaden academic opportunities risk making them more accessible to students who already attend better-resourced institutions.  
The question of subject choice becomes more pronounced at the level of higher education. NEP proposes some of its most far-reaching changes at this stage. Under the University Grants Commission's Curriculum and Credit Framework for Undergraduate Programmes, introduced in 2022, students can pursue three- or four-year undergraduate degrees, combine disciplines through majors and minors, and enter or exit programmes at different stages. Delhi University has adopted a four-year undergraduate structure, with similar reforms envisaged across higher education. Although multidisciplinary education is not a novel concept in India, NEP presents an opportunity to extend its reach beyond the relatively limited number of institutions already offering interdisciplinary programmes, particularly by integrating such flexibility into the public university system. 
Yet, greater flexibility within undergraduate programmes does not necessarily mean greater flexibility in gaining admission to them. While universities are being encouraged to allow students to study across disciplines, their admission requirements continue to depend on subjects chosen in school. Delhi University's 2024 undergraduate admissions guidelines require candidates to appear for CUET in subjects they have studied and passed in Class 12, alongside meeting programme-specific eligibility requirements. For instance, a student who studied Economics but not Mathematics in Class 12 would be ineligible for DU's B.A. (Hons.) Economics programme, which requires Mathematics or Applied Mathematics. Even if the student subsequently developed an interest in pursuing Economics, appearing for the Mathematics paper in CUET would not compensate for the subject's absence from their school curriculum. What appears to be a choice between subjects at sixteen can therefore become a restriction on university options two years later.
University admission prerequisites serve a legitimate academic purpose, ensuring that students possess the foundational knowledge required for particular courses. Their continued rigidity, however, exposes a fundamental inconsistency between the flexibility envisaged by NEP and the eligibility criteria that continue to govern access to higher education. Students are encouraged to explore different academic interests, but admission requirements continue to place considerable weight on decisions made at sixteen. Greater freedom in choosing subjects offers little assurance of corresponding flexibility in university admissions, particularly when students may not fully understand the consequences of their combinations.  
These considerations extend beyond Indian universities, particularly for students planning to pursue higher education abroad. In the United States, undergraduate admissions generally allow greater flexibility in academic backgrounds, but universities continue to evaluate the rigour of a student's school curriculum. Admissions committees evaluate academic preparation in relation to the curricular opportunities available at a student's school, alongside their broader interests and achievements. An interdisciplinary combination such as Biology and Economics may reflect an interest in public health, but an unconventional combination does not necessarily strengthen an application if it comes at the expense of relevant foundational subjects. The freedom to explore therefore still requires students to consider how their choices may be interpreted by universities.          
The United Kingdom presents a more direct example of how subject selection can determine eligibility. Undergraduate admissions through UCAS are often tied to specific course requirements, particularly for specialised degrees. At the London School of Economics, Mathematics is a requirement for admission to the BSc Economics programme. A student who chooses Economics, History and Political Science in Classes 11 and 12, without Mathematics, would be ineligible despite having studied Economics at school. Such prerequisites reflect the academic demands of university courses and cannot reasonably be expected to change in response to India's education reforms. Nevertheless, they illustrate how the choices students make at sixteen can continue to determine which opportunities remain available to them.
Access to informed academic guidance is particularly consequential in this context. Students at schools with dedicated university counsellors are better positioned to navigate domestic and international admission requirements and anticipate the implications of their subject choices. For those without comparable support, these decisions are often made with an incomplete understanding of the opportunities they may foreclose. The expansion of curricular flexibility could thus exacerbate existing educational disparities, as the ability to make informed academic choices remains disproportionately concentrated among students in better-resourced institutions.
NEP marks a substantial shift from the rigid disciplinary divisions that have historically characterised Indian education. Yet the success of these reforms will depend less on the flexibility prescribed in policy documents than on the capacity of institutions to implement it. Unequal school resources, restrictive admission requirements and disparities in academic guidance continue to determine the choices available to students. Unless these institutional constraints are addressed, the expansion of academic freedom risks reinforcing the very inequalities it was intended to diminish.
