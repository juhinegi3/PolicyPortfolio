title: What Good Is Choice When the System Sets the Limits?
date: 2024-06-24
summary: The curriculum now allows subject choice across streams. Schools and universities have been slower to follow.
---

For decades, Indian students have been asked to narrow their academic interests at sixteen, only to be encouraged to rediscover them when applying to university. The choice between science, commerce and humanities has shaped much more than what students study in Classes 11 and 12. It has also determined which degrees they can pursue, which careers they can enter and, increasingly, which universities abroad they can apply to. [The National Education Policy (NEP) of 2020](https://education.gov.in/sites/upload_files/mhrd/files/nep_update/National_Education_Policy_2020_en.pdf) sought to dismantle these divisions. Four years later, the curriculum has undergone considerable revision. The institutions through which students move, however, have been slower to change.

NEP promises greater freedom in choosing subjects, yet much of the education system continues to operate around the old division of streams. Schools must have the teachers and facilities to offer different combinations. Universities must be willing to recognise them. At present, neither is assured. A student may be free to choose subjects under the new framework and still find that the options available at school, or the admission requirements of a university, leave little room for that choice.

The changes to the school curriculum are substantial. [The National Curriculum Framework for School Education, released in 2023](https://dsel.education.gov.in/sites/default/files/update/ncf_2023.pdf), proposes removing the formal separation of Classes 11 and 12 into science, commerce and humanities. Students would be able to combine subjects across disciplines, while examinations would place greater emphasis on understanding and applying concepts. Some of these changes have already reached the examination hall. For the 2024–25 academic session, the Central Board of Secondary Education [increased the proportion of competency-based questions in Classes 11 and 12 from 40 to 50 per cent](https://www.cbseacademic.nic.in/web_material/Circulars/2024/30_Circular_2024.pdf). The Ministry of Education has also established [PARAKH](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/about-parakh), a national assessment centre intended to bring greater consistency to how learning is evaluated across school boards. These are significant changes for a system in which preparation has long revolved around textbooks, memorisation and performance in final examinations.

There is, however, a considerable distance between allowing students to choose subjects and giving them access to those subjects. [The Annual Status of Education Report (ASER) 2023](https://asercentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/ASER-2023_Main-findings-1.pdf) found that 55.7 per cent of rural students enrolled in Class 11 and above were studying arts and humanities. It would be easy to interpret this as a preference for these disciplines. Yet in many rural higher secondary schools, humanities is the only stream available. Offering physics or chemistry requires laboratories, equipment and specialised teachers, resources that many schools struggle to provide. A student interested in combining physics with economics may therefore find that neither her school nor another school nearby offers the subjects she wants. The curriculum may have done away with streams, but the school timetable still reflects the resources available. For many students, little has changed.

The consequences become more apparent when students reach higher education. Undergraduate programmes account for the vast majority of enrolment in Indian universities. According to the [All India Survey on Higher Education (AISHE) 2021–22](https://education.gov.in/sites/upload_files/mhrd/files/statistics-new/AISHE%20Book_2021-22_4.pdf), nearly four in five students in higher education were enrolled at the undergraduate level, with arts accounting for the largest share of enrolment across disciplines at 34.2 per cent. NEP proposes some of its most far-reaching changes at precisely this stage. Under the [University Grants Commission's 2022 framework](https://news.careers360.com/ugc-announces-new-fyup-curriculum-framework-multiple-entry-exit-flexible-ug-degree-options-university/amp), students can pursue three- or four-year degrees with multiple entry and exit points, combine majors and minors across disciplines, and progress from a four-year honours degree [directly to doctoral study](https://news.ulektz.com/2022/12/15/graduates-can-directly-join-phd-programmes-under-new-pattern-ugc-chief/). [Delhi University](https://news.careers360.com/delhi-university-approves-implementation-of-nep-from-session-2022-23/amp) has adopted the framework, and other institutions are beginning to follow.

India is already familiar with the idea of a broad undergraduate education. NEP itself draws on an older Indian tradition of learning across disciplines, while several private universities have offered liberal arts and interdisciplinary degrees for years. Their reach, however, has remained limited. Such programmes are often expensive and concentrated in a few cities, making them inaccessible to a large proportion of students. NEP could change this by bringing similar opportunities into public universities, where far more students pursue their degrees at considerably lower costs. A student interested in economics and computer science, for instance, could also study philosophy or political theory without having to treat those interests as separate from her formal education.

But even as universities begin to offer more flexible degrees, admission into them remains governed by requirements that frequently reflect the old stream system. The Common University Entrance Test (CUET), for which [more than 13 lakh candidates registered in 2024](https://university.careers360.com/articles/how-many-students-appeared-for-cuet-2024), is an important part of this transition. Universities use subject-specific examination scores and eligibility requirements to determine which undergraduate programmes students can enter. Where admission to a degree depends on a prescribed combination of school subjects, students who have taken advantage of NEP's flexibility may find themselves ineligible. Someone who chose biology alongside economics and history could discover that her combination excludes her from a programme she wishes to pursue. Such restrictions may be justified where a course requires particular foundational knowledge. In other cases, they risk preserving divisions that the school curriculum is trying to remove. Students need to know which is which before making their choices in Class 11.

The issue extends to students considering universities abroad. According to the Ministry of External Affairs, [more than 13 lakh Indian students were studying overseas in 2024](https://www.theweek.in/wire-updates/national/2024/08/01/del121-rsq-mea-indian-students.html). India has also become [the largest source of international students in the United States](https://opendoorsdata.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/OD24_Presentation_Print_2024-11-12.pdf). Much of this movement, however, takes place after undergraduate education. [The latest Open Doors report](https://opendoorsdata.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/OpenDoors_FactSheet_India_2024.pdf) recorded approximately 36,000 Indian undergraduate students in the United States, compared with nearly 197,000 graduate students. Undergraduate enrolment grew by around 13 per cent over the previous year, suggesting growing interest in pursuing a bachelor's degree overseas. Yet the overall expansion of Indian student mobility has been driven largely by postgraduate study and opportunities for employment after graduation. It would be premature to connect these figures to NEP. Students educated under the new school curriculum framework have yet to enter university.

Even so, the implications for future applicants are worth considering. Undergraduate admissions in the United States [generally follow a holistic model](https://brynmawr.edu/stories/what-do-we-mean-holistic-application-review). Universities consider academic performance alongside the difficulty of a student's coursework, extracurricular involvement, recommendations and evidence of intellectual curiosity. A student who has studied economics and biology, for example, may be able to demonstrate an interest in the relationship between public health and economic policy. NEP could give students more opportunities to explore such connections during school, and to develop academic interests that extend beyond a conventional stream.

The situation is different in the United Kingdom, where students typically [apply to a particular degree programme](https://www.imperial.ac.uk/study/help-centre/undergraduate-admissions/how-do-i-apply-for-undergraduate-study.php) and admission requirements are closely tied to the subject they intend to study. [Mathematics is required for entry into many economics and engineering programmes](https://lse.ac.uk/study-at-lse/Undergraduate/degree-programmes-2024/BSc-Economics/Home.aspx), irrespective of what other subjects an applicant may have pursued. A student who has selected an unusual combination of subjects under NEP may therefore find herself well suited to the broader academic expectations of some American universities while being ineligible for certain British degrees. These distinctions matter, especially when decisions made at sixteen can determine the programmes available to a student two years later. Families often become aware of such requirements only when applications begin, by which point changing subjects is rarely possible.

The same problem runs through all three routes into higher education: public universities in India, domestic liberal arts institutions and universities abroad. Students are being encouraged to pursue a wider range of academic interests, while the institutions they hope to enter continue to place different, and sometimes conflicting, conditions on their choices. There is little value in offering students flexibility without ensuring that they understand how those choices will be treated later.

An immediate priority should be making subject availability more transparent. State governments and education boards could publish the subjects offered by individual higher secondary schools, allowing families to understand which combinations are realistically available in their area. Such information would also make it possible to identify districts where limited teachers or infrastructure prevent schools from offering the choices envisaged by NEP. Without this information, the extent of the gap between policy and provision remains difficult to measure.

University admissions requirements also warrant closer examination. Institutions and the National Testing Agency should review subject prerequisites for individual undergraduate programmes, retaining those that reflect genuine academic requirements and reconsidering those inherited from conventional stream divisions. Where advanced mathematics or another subject is essential, students should be informed well before selecting their Class 11 subjects. Clearer guidance would allow them to explore different disciplines without unknowingly closing off degrees they may later wish to pursue.

Schools and education counsellors have a responsibility here as well. Subject selection should begin with a student's academic interests, but it must also take account of the requirements imposed by universities in India and abroad. A student interested in both literature and mathematics should have the opportunity to study them together. She should also know whether dropping physics would affect her eligibility for engineering, or whether a particular British economics programme expects further mathematical preparation. These conversations need to happen before subject combinations are finalised.

Four years after NEP was introduced, much of the work remains at the institutional level. Revising curriculum frameworks and examination patterns has been an important beginning. Schools still need the staff and infrastructure to offer the combinations those frameworks permit, and universities need admission rules that can accommodate students arriving with more varied academic backgrounds. Until those changes take hold, students will continue to make their choices within a system whose boundaries remain considerably narrower than the curriculum suggests.
