Dashboard project, part 3

Policy analysis

Are improvements in healthcare access translating into better care and nutrition? NFHS-5 (2019–21) vs NFHS-6 (2023–24), Bihar and Odisha, 68 districts.

1. Check-ups and iron tablets 2. Care at the facility and at home 3. Stunting and wasting

1.Bihar: More check-ups have not led to more iron tablet use

In Bihar, the share of mothers with four or more antenatal check-ups rose 11.6 points between the two surveys. However, the share taking iron-folic acid tablets for 180 days (the full recommended course) rose only 3.0 points.

If more visits led to more tablet use, districts that added the most visits should show the largest gains in tablet use. Across Bihar's 38 districts there is no relationship between the two.

Odisha shows the two can move separately. Its tablet use rose 17.3 points, to about 52 in every 100 mothers, without any rise in check-ups. Within Odisha, the districts that added the most visits also tended to add the most tablet use, so the two moved together there even though the state average of visits fell. Both states run the same national programme for iron tablets, so the difference is likely in how it is delivered: whether tablets are in stock locally, and whether health workers check at home that mothers are taking them.

Recommendation

Bihar should not assume that raising attendance further will raise tablet use. It should look at how states where tablet use rose, such as Odisha, supply tablets and follow up with mothers, and start with the Bihar districts where visits rose most but tablet use stayed flat.

2.Bihar: Care has improved in the facility, but not at home

Babies breastfed within an hour of birth rose 20.5 points and improved in all 38 districts. Children aged 6 to 23 months getting an adequate diet, meaning enough meals and enough variety of food for their age, rose only 1.6 points and fell in 13 districts.

The difference between the two metrics is that early breastfeeding happens in the hospital, at birth, with nurses present to help. A child's diet is decided at home, every day, for months after the family has left the facility. What the health system does directly has improved however what depends on families alone has not.

About 77% of children in a typical Bihar district are fully vaccinated by 12–23 months, which takes several health system visits in the first year. So most families are already in regular contact with the health system during the months when feeding begins. And since 2018, a government programme has sent ASHA workers to homes when a child is 3, 6, 9, 12 and 15 months old, partly to advise on feeding. That programme had run for about five years by the time of the second survey, which is long enough to expect some effect. Yet diets barely changed.

That leaves two likely explanations, but we cannot be sure which applies:

  • The home visits are not happening as planned, or the advice given is not making a difference.
  • Families know what to feed their children but cannot afford it. A varied diet needs foods such as eggs, milk, pulses and fruit, which cost more than staple grains.

Recommendation

In the 13 districts where diets fell, the state should first establish which of these is the barrier, by checking whether home visits are being completed and whether families can afford the foods they are advised to give. If visits are not happening, the fix lies in how ASHA workers are supported and supervised. If cost is the barrier, increased food support such as take-home rations would need to be provided.

3.Odisha: Falling stunting is hiding rising wasting

Stunting (when a child is too short for their age) fell in 24 of Odisha's 30 districts. In 18 of those 24, wasting (child is too thin for their height) rose over the same period.

Stunting builds up over years of poor nutrition, so it falls slowly as long-term conditions improve. Wasting can develop within weeks, after a shortage of food or an illness, and it is the more immediately dangerous of the two. A state can make long-term progress on stunting while children are going hungry in the short term.

Children's diets also fell in most of Odisha's districts, including 14 of these 18. But the districts where diets fell most were not the ones where wasting rose most, so diet alone does not explain the rise. The national nutrition mission sets reduction goals for stunting, underweight, anaemia and low birth weight. Wasting is recorded, but it is not one of those goals. A rise in wasting can therefore go unremarked while stunting improves.

Recommendation

Odisha should not read its fall in stunting as a sign that its nutrition approach is working everywhere. In these 18 districts, the priority should be finding thin children early, through the monthly weighing anganwadis already carry out, and getting them treated quickly. Wasting needs a fast response, and the long-term measures that reduce stunting will not deliver one.
